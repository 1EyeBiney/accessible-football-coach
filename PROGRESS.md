## Milestone 1: the staff knowledge model

Built engine/staff.js, the file that owns everything the coach hears from another person. It implements the staff attributes in section 5.2, the hunches of section 5.3, the spotter and the substitution flow of section 18, the trainer of 18.4, the report urgencies of section 19, the never-omniscient rule of 24.1, and the observed-events model of 26.7.

A staff member is a record with a role, a name, a personality, and attributes on the same one to ninety-nine scale the players use. Coordinators carry evaluation, scheme, communication, teaching, loyalty, and ambition. The spotter carries eyes, timing, accuracy, and voice. The trainer carries diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. The spotter's eyes does the work evaluation does for a coordinator, and his timing does the work communication does, and that mapping lives in exactly one place in the file so it cannot drift.

Each staff member gets a belief store for the game. The store files observations against short matchup keys: one per receiver role, one per run point of attack, one for protection as a whole, one for the quarterback escaping the pocket, and one for the screen game. The offensive coordinator's store is about his own offense; the defensive coordinator's store uses the same keys about the offense he is facing. Nothing goes into a store except what came off the events on a play result, plus pre-game scouting if any is handed in, which is the rule from section 24.1.

Two attributes do the work, and they were deliberately made to work differently. Evaluation sets a bias that is drawn once per matchup per game and never averages away, so a poor evaluator is persistently wrong about one particular player rather than randomly jittery about all of them, which is how a real coach is wrong. It also sets how many looks he needs before he will say anything: a good coordinator draws his conclusion in three plays and a poor one needs closer to ten. Communication sets how many snaps pass between knowing something and telling you. A hunch comes out in the shape the contract in CLAUDE.md describes, with a source, a kind, a target, a confidence word, a recommendation, the evidence behind it, an urgency, and a plain sentence, and a test checks that a hunch never speaks a number.

Both automatic coaches were rewired onto this. The offensive coach no longer reads a raw counter of what he has seen; he asks his coordinator what coverage this defense likes on this kind of down, and calls into it through the scheme matrix. The defensive coach no longer reads an exploitation counter; the counter loop of section 8.3 now runs entirely off the defensive coordinator's belief store, so a coordinator who has not worked out what is happening to him simply never adjusts, which is the intended cost of a weak staff. Substitution recommendations are generated from the coordinator's watch on his own side's stamina, the automatic coach answers them, and the player actually leaves the field and comes back when he is fresh, announced at the next change of possession as section 18.3 requires.

Three things had to be fixed along the way, because the first version of the model did not pass its own test. They are worth recording because they were real problems and not tuning.

The first was what a coordinator observes. He was originally filing the raw separation the play produced, which mixes together the man beating his defender, the scheme, the coverage shell, and the help. That is not a stable thing to have an opinion about, so nobody could form a useful one. The engine now carries the matchup edge on the target event separately from the separation, with the scheme and the help stripped out, and the coordinator forms his belief from that. This is exactly what section 5.3 describes: the engine knows the true edge, and what you hear is that edge filtered through the man telling you.

The second was the quarterback. He was picking the best open receiver on every snap regardless of what the play was designed to do, which made him a perfect quarterback and made the order of a concept's reads meaningless. He now works the progression the concept defines, in order, and throws the first read that is open enough, accepting less separation the deeper into the progression he goes, and paying in time for every read he passes on. This is a better reading of section 26.3 and it is what makes a concept's design matter. It also fixed a hole that was visible in the baseline: bracketing the X receiver used to have no measurable effect against Four Verticals, because the quarterback simply threw somewhere else. A bracket now costs the offense two yards a snap on that play and safety help costs three, so the counter loop has teeth.

The third was that no coordinator could read a defense, because the defense picked a coverage at close to random on every snap and there was nothing there to read. Defensive staffs now have a tendency by down and distance, some of them heavily patterned and some of them mixing it up, and the offensive coordinator learns those tendencies by situation rather than in the aggregate. He can also misread a shell, and when he does he misreads it plausibly, calling a one-high look the wrong one-high look rather than inventing something wild. How often that happens is set by his evaluation. This is the tendency tracking that sections 8.3 and 16.5 call for and the code did not have.

The harness now reports hunch accuracy two ways. The first is the direct test and it is the one that matters: at the final whistle, did the coordinator name the receiver who really was winning his matchup? Over three hundred games a poor coordinator names the right man forty point five percent of the time, and the man he names is on average three point eight three worse than the best one. An average coordinator is right fifty-six point one percent of the time with a miss of one point nine four. A good coordinator is right sixty-eight point five percent of the time with a miss of one point one four. That is a clear gradient and it is the acceptance test the kickoff asked for.

The second measure is honest but weaker, and it needs to be read with care. It asks whether the snaps that attacked the matchup the coordinator named gained more than the same concept gains when it is called with no read behind it. Across all three bands that is worth between a fifth and four fifths of a yard, and the bands do not separate cleanly. The reason is worth writing down. Within a single concept the hunch does not change anything at all, because the concept's reads are fixed; the coordinator's value shows up in which concept gets called, and comparing a concept against itself deliberately removes exactly that. Measured instead as points on the scoreboard between teams with identical rosters and different staff, a good offensive coordinator is worth something like one and a half points a game over a poor one. That is real, but it is small, and making it larger by leaning harder on the scheme matrix made it worse rather than better, because the offense over-commits to one answer and becomes predictable. The weight was set from that curve. This is the clearest open item coming out of the milestone and it is flagged for the balance pass.

## Milestone 2: the game controller

Built engine/controller.js, which is the only thing the interface will ever talk to for a game. It returns strings and plain objects and never touches the page. It implements the play call flow of section 16.5, the play clock of 16.5.1, the substitution flow of 18.3, the report loop of section 19, the three delegation modes of section 22, and halftime and the postgame review of section 23.

To make it possible, engine/game.js was refactored so a game can be walked one step at a time rather than only played end to end. There is now a startGame that sets up and kicks off, a stepGame that advances at most one snap and handles the quarter breaks, halftime, and overtime, and playGame is a short loop over stepGame that keeps the old behaviour for the harness. Overtime is now a small state machine instead of a nested loop, which is what let it be stepped. The harness numbers are unchanged by the refactor, which was the point.

The controller answers one question at a time. Its pending function says what the game needs next: a substitution answer, a halftime choice, an offensive call, a defensive call, nothing because a delegated side is handling it, or the game is over. The suggestion function gives the coordinator's call for either side, with the confidence in words, the description of what the play is good against for a coach who is still learning, and the count of how many times it has been called this season and how often it has worked. It speaks the word sub inside the line when the call changes personnel and hands the defense a free substitution, exactly as 16.5 asks.

The report loop is built to the order in 19.3. The play result comes first, then any must-answer report, then a chime for each source that has something cued waiting, then the situation line when a possession or a quarter has changed, then the coordinator's suggestion. There is a chimes function that says which of the offensive coordinator, defensive coordinator and spotter have something waiting, so the interface can play one sound each, and a reports function that hands over the words when the coach asks for them. Batched notes come out separately. The report threshold of 19.4 filters cued and batched reports and never touches a must-answer one.

The three delegation modes work. In the first the coach calls everything. In the second the coordinator calls everything and the coach just advances. The third is the interesting one and the design expects most coaches to live there: the game runs itself and stops only for third and fourth down, the red zone, the last two minutes of a half, and a coordinator who is sure about something. Measured on a test game, that turns roughly a hundred and thirty snaps into a few dozen decisions.

Halftime produces three things the staff learned, two things the opponent changed, and the biggest personnel problem, all drawn from the coordinators' belief stores rather than from the truth, so a bad staff gives a vague briefing. The five strategic choices shape the second half. The postgame review speaks about each assistant in plain words with no numbers, which is what section 23.2 asks for.

Two real bugs were found and fixed while building it, both worth recording.

The first is that working out a suggestion draws from the random number generator, because the coordinator's choice is a weighted pick. That meant a coach who asked his coordinator twice got a different game from one who asked once, and the seed stopped replaying. Suggestions are now worked out once per snap and remembered until the snap is played.

The second showed up in a trace of a real drive. On fourth down the controller was asking the coach to call a play, and then the engine punted or kicked a field goal and threw the call away. The controller now recognises when the next snap belongs to the special teams, or is a victory formation kneel, and does not ask for a call it is going to ignore. Section 8.4 wants a full special teams suggestion flow eventually; this is the honest interim behaviour rather than a call that silently disappears.

One more change was needed in engine/game.js. The automatic coach answers its own substitution recommendations, which is right for the opponent but wrong for the coach's own team, where the whole point is that he is asked. Teams now carry a flag saying whether a person is coaching them, and the coach's substitution questions are left for him and surfaced as the must-answer report of 19.2.

test/controller_test.js was written as the kickoff describes. A scripted coach plays whole games through the controller, calling both sides on every snap from the suggestions and answering everything the game asks. It checks that the game reaches a final, that a full play by play is produced, that every announcement is a non-empty string carrying a known priority, that nothing a member of staff says contains a digit anywhere, that the situation line speaks its numbers as words, and that the same seed replays every play by play line word for word while a different seed does not. It also plays a fully delegated game and a stop-for-the-big-ones game to check both reach a final, and checks the formation list, the call sheet, the substitution list, the examine line, the matchup key, the tendency key, and the play clock including the delay of game penalty.

## Milestone 3: the playable shell

The game now opens and plays. index.html, ui/core.js, ui/help_text.js, ui/dom.js, ui/screens.js and main.js are all in place, built to the Accessible Golf model in section 21, and a full game can be played from the boot button to the postgame review without a mouse.

The split is the one CLAUDE.md asks for. ui/core.js holds everything that does not touch the page: the announce queue and its priorities, the speech sanitiser, the menu and grid state machines, the point allocation list, the confirmation flow, the help viewer, and the ordered interceptor stack. All of it runs under Node and all of it is tested there. ui/dom.js is the only file in the project that touches document, and it owns the focus trap, the single live region, the visual mirror, the chimes, and reading and writing files. ui/help_text.js holds every word of help and every key description. ui/screens.js holds the screens and the keys that work on each. main.js does nothing but wire them together and install the one keydown listener.

The focus trap works as the design describes. The page is an application region with one button reading Press Enter to Begin. Pressing it creates the audio context, which browsers only allow inside a click, hides the button, shows the container and gives it focus. A focus listener puts focus back in the container if anything takes it. F5, F6 and F11 pass through to the browser and so do control R and control F; everything else is handled and prevented.

There is one voice. Everything spoken goes through a single sr-only div with an assertive live region, and every visible part of the page is aria-hidden so the screen reader hears exactly one channel. Everything is sanitised on the way out, so third and seven is spoken as third and seven, a minus sign becomes the word minus, and a percent sign becomes the word percent.

The chimes are synthesised with oscillators, so there are no sound files to ship. There is one for each source, as section 19.2 requires: a higher tone for the offensive coordinator, a lower one for the defensive coordinator, a bright one for the spotter, and a heavier one for anything that must be answered, plus the small ticks for moving through a list and opening and closing a viewer.

The screens are the ones the kickoff lists and no more. The boot button, a main menu, a team choice between two authored high school programmes described entirely in words with no numbers anywhere, a pre-game screen that names your staff and lets you set delegation and the play clock, and the game screen with the full play call flow: the coordinator suggests first, Enter accepts, F opens the formation list and then the call sheet for this down and distance, N is no huddle at the formation prompt, U opens the substitution list, and the defense uses the same keys through front, coverage, pressure and adjustment. Tab, C, X, M, R, S, T, P, V and B all work. Halftime and the final with the postgame review are in. Saving writes the play by play to a file; saving a whole run is not built.

Three real bugs were found and fixed, and the last of them is the reason it is worth testing an interface rather than reading it.

The first was that engine/controller.js drains its own queue and returns what was said, and ui/screens.js was ignoring the return value and then draining again, which came back empty. The result was that every single play result was silently thrown away. A whole game produced a hundred and thirty spoken lines for a hundred and thirty four snaps, all of them suggestions, and not one description of what had actually happened. The headless test caught it as soon as it counted the lines.

The second was that the substitution list stays open after a substitution, which is right, because a coach may want to make several. That is fine behaviour but it needed Escape to be documented and it needed the test to use it.

The third only showed up in a real browser, and it was the important one. The announce queue was written to speak one line and then wait long enough for it to be heard before speaking the next. That is the wrong model for a live region, because the screen reader is already reading at its own speed, and it means the queue backs up behind itself. Driving a full game in the browser produced three spoken lines and a backlog of several hundred. Everything a keystroke produces is now spoken as one utterance in priority order, which is what one voice actually means, and pacing has been moved to where the design puts it: the delay before the game does something the coach did not ask for. That also made the delegation modes real, because a side handed to a coordinator now advances on its own between snaps rather than waiting for a key.

Terse mode was added while the play by play was being wired, which is the improvement the kickoff lists as the thing to do with spare time. Every snap now carries two forms. The full one gives the call, the coverage, the pressure, the adjustment and who beat whom. The terse one gives the down, the distance, the spot and what happened, which is the one line a play that section 2 asks for, and V switches between them at any time.

Two test files were added. test/ui_core_test.js exercises the pure logic: the announce queue ordering including that a late must-answer does not jump a result already waiting, menu wrap in both directions, grid edge announcements on all four sides, the interceptor priority including that a key pressed while help is open never reaches the game and that an open viewer swallows a key it does not want, the confirmation flow, the help headings, the keyboard explorer table, and the Focus allocation rule, which refuses to raise a category when nothing is spare and says so rather than quietly taking points from a category the coach is not on. test/shell_test.js loads every file index.html loads, in the same order, and plays whole games by sending keystrokes, checking that a game finishes, that nothing spoken is empty or contains the word undefined or a raw ordinal or a percent sign, that quitting asks first and can be cancelled, that Escape always says where the coach has ended up, and that no snap is ever played while a list is open.

The browser was also driven directly, over a local server, to check the parts no Node test can reach: the scripts load and register, the button click creates the audio context and moves focus into the container, the live region and the visual mirror are both written, and a full game of a hundred and sixty three snaps produces a hundred and sixty six spoken utterances with no backlog.

Verification before the commit: node harness.js 100 1 --even inside its targets, node matrix.js clean, node test/run.js at two hundred and forty four checks across four files, all passing, and every JavaScript file passes a syntax check.

## Milestone 3, second pass: the accessibility audit

The accessibility auditor walked every interactive state key by key and came back with nineteen findings. Fourteen were fixed the same night, four were already correct, and the rest are recorded in REVIEW_NOTES.md. Several of them would have made the game unusable rather than merely awkward, and they are worth reading.

The worst was the pacing timer. It checked only whether a delegated side was waiting, and never whether the coach had anything open. So it pressed Enter into whatever was in front of him. Press Q to quit, think about it for a moment, and the timer answered the confirmation for you and threw the game away. Press F1 on any fourth down and help closed itself mid-sentence while a punt ran. Turn on the keyboard explorer during a delegated drive and it repeated the same line every few seconds forever. The timer now refuses to fire while a confirmation, help, a list, or the explorer is open.

The second was silence. Any key that no handler wanted produced nothing at all: no speech, no sound, nothing. That is the one thing the design says is always a bug, and it had a second edge to it. Every letter handler compares against a lower case letter, so with Caps Lock on, which is the NVDA laptop modifier and gets toggled by accident, the entire game went mute. Single characters are now folded to lower case before they reach any handler, so Caps Lock is harmless, and a key nothing wanted now says so and points at F1.

The third was that Escape lied. The context the game remembered was set on the menu screens and never updated once a game started, so every Escape and every list closing announced "Back at the pre-game screen" for the whole night. Escape now speaks the live situation, and if the coach still owes an answer it says so and repeats the suggestion he was on.

Several keys were documented and dead. Shift H, which the very first line of help promises moves to the previous heading, could never fire, because the browser reports a shifted H as a capital letter and the handler was looking for a lower case one. Shift G was dead the same way. F1 did not close help and could not be opened from inside a list, which made the help for lists unreachable, and F12 was swallowed in both places, so the keyboard explorer could not be turned on in the two places a learner would most want it. The formation list spoke a prompt offering N for no huddle and U for substitutions and handled neither, even though section 16.5 puts both of them at exactly that prompt.

The repeat key had been quietly ruined. C is the only way a screen reader user can hear something again, because he cannot scroll back, and every single utterance was overwriting it, including "Closed" and "Pacing slow". After arrowing through the call sheet, C would repeat the word "Closed". Only football goes into the repeat buffer now.

The quick keys were also one layer too far down the stack. Tab, C, P, V and Q all lived under the help and list layers, so while the substitution list was open the coach could not check the situation, could not re-hear the last line, and could not quit, even though help calls all five the keys that work everywhere. There is now a global layer above help and the lists, and they work everywhere as advertised.

Two smaller ones were worth fixing for the same reason. The final screen said "Press Enter to return to the menu" before the postgame review, because the instruction was at a higher priority than the review lines, which invited the coach to press Enter and lose the verdicts on his own assistants. And Escape from the call sheet abandoned the formation choice entirely rather than going back one step, which is what section 16.5 asks for.

The play clock was a setting that did nothing: the controller exposed the seconds and the penalty, and nothing ever ran a timer. It now runs, with a soft tick from ten seconds and a sharper one from five, and a delay of game costs five yards and replays the down. It also stops whenever anything is open, for the same reason the pacing timer does.

Explore mode was lying about the five keys that belong to the browser. F5, F6, F11, control R and control F bypassed the whole stack, so a learner exploring the keyboard could press F5 and lose the game, while the explorer told him the key did nothing. Those keys still reach the browser in normal play, which is the point of them, but while the explorer is on they are described rather than obeyed, and they are now written down in help along with a warning that a game in progress is not saved.

The download link and the file picker both needed work. The link is transient and there is no other way to start a download from a static page, but it is now hidden from the screen reader, kept out of the tab order, and focus is returned to the container afterwards. The file picker leaked: dismissing the native dialog fires no event at all, so the input stayed in the document forever, one per attempt, focus never came back, and the coach was left in silence not knowing where he was. It now notices the dialog closing and cleans up.

One more thing came out of testing the fixes rather than out of the audit, and it was the biggest simulation bug of the night. Nobody ever got tired. Stamina drained by two point four a snap and recovered by three point two, and since a unit is on the field about half the time, every player gained stamina over the course of a game. The lowest figure on a roster after a full game was eighty two, the substitution floor is under forty, and so not one substitution was ever requested in any game ever played. The entire substitution flow of section 18.3, the gassed reports, the fresh legs against the defense resetting trade, and the recovered player announcements were all dead code that had never once executed. Recovery is now slower than the drain, a starting lineman is in the fifties or sixties by the fourth quarter, and a game now produces between ten and eighteen substitution requests. Finding that also turned up a second bug in the same area: the branch that leaves the substitution question for a human coach was skipping the line that puts the hunch in the report queue, so even once players did get tired, the coach would never have been asked.

Verified again in the browser afterwards: a hundred and fifty nine snaps producing a hundred and seventy nine distinct utterances, eighteen substitution requests answered, no announcement containing undefined, a raw ordinal, or a percent sign, and the postgame review spoken before the invitation to leave.

## Milestone 4: the balance pass and the morning report

The balance runner played two thousand games across four configurations and read five of them line by line. It could not write its own notes, so its findings are recorded in BALANCE_NOTES.md by hand, along with what was done about each one and the numbers before and after. Twelve things were fixed, and the rest are written down with measurements rather than impressions.

The most important thing it found was a crash. node harness.js 300 11 died reproducibly, and so did seed 13, at a rate of about one game in three thousand. Both quarterbacks on one roster were injured out of the same game, the lineup builder had nobody to pick, and it handed a null to the snap resolver. If a position is wiped out entirely, somebody else now plays there, taken from the nearest position that can cover it. A high school team that loses both quarterbacks puts an athlete under centre; it does not field ten men. Both seeds now complete three hundred games.

Six things a listener would actually hear were wrong and are fixed. Overtime printed a game clock of a hundred and sixty six minutes and counted down from it, because the untimed sentinel was being formatted as a clock; overtime is now silent on the clock and its periods are spoken in words rather than mixing period one with period 2. The line to gain ran away from the ball, because a penalty near the goal line moved the ball only half the distance but added the full ten yards to the distance, so a series read second and twenty five, then second and thirty five, then second and forty five while the ball moved a few yards; the half the distance rule is now enforced and the distance moves only as far as the ball does, which also removes the first and thirty and second and forty four lines that do not happen in football. Goal to go stuck to a series after a penalty put the ball outside the ten. A kickoff returned all the way was credited to the kicking team and never named the team that scored, which for a game read aloud says the opposite of what happened. A safety produced no free kick line at all. And thirty nine percent of penalty announcements left out the yardage, because holding and pass interference never stated it.

Four of the five defensive adjustments were inert or backwards, and that was a fault introduced earlier the same night. Bracketing the best receiver was making the defense a yard a snap worse and safety help eight tenths worse, because the bonus given to the other receivers was larger than the penalty on the man being doubled. The bonus is now smaller than the penalty and both adjustments help the defense. Contain did nothing at all to the run game, which is its only purpose, and now costs an outside run over a yard a carry. Loading the box was the one that already worked and now works harder. The spy is still weak: it cuts the scramble rate but scrambles are rare enough that it does not show in yards allowed, and it is the most called adjustment in the game. That is logged rather than fixed.

Bringing more rushers used to be strictly better with no cost anywhere, and cover zero was the best coverage in the game while being called on four percent of snaps. A coverage with nobody deep now gives up far more explosive plays, on the ground and through the air, which is the actual price of an all out blitz. Cover zero has moved from the best coverage to the fourth. Six rushers is still the best pressure, but the gap has narrowed from nine tenths of a yard to two thirds and some of what is left is selection rather than balance, since the automatic coach blitzes on obvious passing downs. Better, not finished.

Fumbles lost were running at forty percent of their target. Both the carry and the catch fumble chances were raised, and fumbles lost are now around six tenths a game against a target of about one. That is deliberately still short, because a fumble a side every game reads as slapstick.

## Milestone 5a: save and load

Brian asked to have what was already sitting in ISSUES.md addressed before his next round of play testing, rather than wait for a batch of play notes: save and load, the special teams flow, and the tuning items. Save and load first, since KICKOFF-2.md put it first and the special teams flow does not depend on it.

The hard part was never the JSON; it was that a player is not owned by one place in the state. He is referenced from the roster that generated him, from a substitution hunch's target, from an event's carrier or tackler or rusher or blocker, and from up to four pieces of evidence a belief store keeps per matchup. engine/controller.js's answerSubstitution mutates a hunch's target player directly (`p.live.benched = true`), so if a reload turned every one of those references into its own disconnected copy instead of the same live object, a substitution made after loading would silently stop showing up in the roster everywhere else read it. That is exactly the kind of wrongness CLAUDE.md says to treat as a bug.

engine/save.js is the fix: a player is written once, in the roster that owns him, and every other reference to him anywhere in the tree is replaced with a tagged id (`{ $p: id }`) before the JSON is written. Loading rebuilds both rosters first, builds one id-to-player map across both of them (ids are unique across a game because the roster generator stamps the team name into every id), and then walks the rest of the saved tree resolving every tag back into the real object. A belief store's `member` is put back by hand rather than trusted through the tag system, since a staff member is not a player and is never tagged; without that line a reloaded coordinator would answer questions from a disconnected copy of himself. The other thing worth recording: `controller.suggestCache` is saved on purpose, not dropped as clutter. Asking a coordinator for his suggestion draws from the game's seed, and the cache is what stops asking twice from drawing twice; dropping it on reload would let the loaded game redraw a suggestion it had already made and run one random draw ahead of an unbroken game for the rest of the night. The only two things genuinely left out are the one cycle in the state (`game.controller` points back at the controller; the load side reattaches it by hand) and the transient fields that are always empty between decisions anyway (`hooks`, `queue`, `forcedOffense`, `forcedDefense`).

## Session 4, August 29, 2026: the coin toss and the kickoff

Milestone 10, from the last item in Brian's play notes, built after he accepted proposal 4 in conversation. The game no longer starts itself: every game opens at the coin toss, and every kickoff all game long is a call.

The structural move that made it possible: kickoffs used to resolve synchronously inside whatever scored - the touchdown path, the field goal path, the halftime rollover, even a kickoff returned for a score kicked off again recursively. They are now deferred: kickoff() just records that one is owed, and stepGame resolves it as a step of its own before anything else, exactly the pattern quarter breaks already used. The four call sites did not change at all, and because the deferral moves the draws without reordering them, a headless game's rng stream is identical to the old one wherever the calls come out the same. The toss got the same treatment: startGame marks it pending, the first step resolves it, and the headless path makes the same single draw the old startGame made with the same meaning, so seeds replay.

On top of that: the toss flow (call it in the air, winner chooses receive, defer, or kick - defend-an-end deliberately withheld until the engine has wind for it to mean anything), the kickoff calls (deep, squib, pooch, onside kicking; regular return or hands team receiving), and a real onside mechanic, roughly one in five recovered against a return unit and half that against a hands team. The desperation window - fourth quarter, inside four minutes, down one to sixteen - is deterministic and public information, so the computer coach kicks onside from it, the computer receiver sends the hands team from it, and a KEY-mode human is stopped exactly when it is live. The gating reuses the fourth-down precedent: offenseMode, with COORD never asked and the toss the one exception that always asks, because it happens once and it is the coach's moment.

Proposal 4's accepted seed also landed: the controller counts coach-made against staff-made decisions per game (a forced call on the coach's side is his, his side resolving without one is his staff's, each kickoff carries exactly one such decision, the toss deliberately none), and the count rides through saves. ## Session 5, Milestone 12: how players are named

The fourth thing from Brian's notes, and the largest. The play by play built its event sentences out of bare player names - "Marcus Webb beat Terrell Jones" - which is the one thing a coach who cannot see the field has nothing to anchor: no jersey number, no replay, no way to look a name up mid-drive. The codebase had solved this once and never carried it across, because the coordinator hunches in engine/staff.js already spoke in roles, which is the voice DESIGN.md 5.3 writes into its own examples.

Brian chose the shape before any code was written: position and last name by default, with position only and name only as the alternatives, and specific authored labels rather than generic group names, knowing that the engine models position groups and fills them off the depth chart rather than modelling alignment.

The labels went into engine/players.js next to POSITIONS, which already owns what a player is. Two facts made the convention defensible rather than arbitrary, and both were already implicit in code nobody had read that way: the run concepts pair inside runs to the second, third and fourth line slots and off-tackle runs to the fourth and fifth, which is the interior three and then one edge, so left tackle through right tackle is how the engine already uses those slots; and coverage already sends the X receiver to the first defensive back and the Z to the second, so corner, corner, safety, safety, nickel, dime falls straight out. The lineup builders stamp the label on each man as they build, so resolve.js can name anybody without carrying a lineup around, and engine/staff.js's own copy of the role vocabulary was folded into the same table so the two cannot drift.

Making the setting apply to lines already spoken was the interesting part. Verbosity works retroactively because the engine stores both a full and a terse form and the controller picks between them at read time; storing six renderings to get the same behaviour for three naming modes would have been silly. So a line that still carries its result is rebuilt from that result instead, and each event keeps the shape it was written in ("$1 beat $2") alongside the men it names. That is what lets pressing A say the last snap again in the setting just chosen, which is the fastest way to pick the one you want.

Two bugs of my own along the way, both caught before the reviewer. The first is worth recording because of how quiet it was: the regular expression for pulling a last name was called LAST, which is already the array of surnames the roster generator picks from, so every player in the game was suddenly named "undefined undefined" - the shadowing was silent, and the suite caught it in one run. The second was cosmetic and only visible in the browser: a position label starting a sentence reads in lower case where a name did not, so "running back Dawson for 8" needed capitalising.

The reviewer found eight real things and the first was the important one: the labels did not actually stay with a man. Both lineup builders indexed the list of who was available rather than the depth chart, so resting one player for a series renamed everybody behind him - 4.6 players a game called two different things, and the help text promised the coach the exact opposite in as many words. Defensive labels now come off the full depth chart and the instability is down to 0.12 a game, which is a man genuinely promoted when the starter above him is hurt. The offense is deliberately not the same and now says so in three places: an offensive slot is a real job, so a back filling in while the starter rests really is the running back while he is out there.

The rest of the reviewer's list, all fixed: the timeout clause went silent about twice a game the moment lines started being rebuilt, because it was appended to the stored strings after the description had already been built, so a coach late in a close game was never told his opponent had stopped the clock; re-rendering an old line borrowed where players stand now rather than where they stood then, which moved a fifth of the blocks in a re-rendered log to the wrong linemen; name-only mode said "Fletcher beat Fletcher" about twice a game, since fifty surnames are shared across eighty men, and now says the whole name; pressing A announced the setting and re-read nothing, which is not what the rebuildable sentences were built for; the substitution list said "DL Marcus Webb", read out as the letters D and L, in precisely the one place a coach maps a name to a position; and six more log lines were missing the full stop that keeps them from running into the next sentence, the touchdown into the extra point being the frequent one. One pre-existing thing was fixed because it is one line and audible in every game ever played: the situation line said "three quarter" where it meant "third quarter".

Two things were left and written into ISSUES.md with their measurements. Special teams name nobody in any mode, so a coach never meets his kicker, punter or returner - a gap in the idea rather than a defect in it. And the offensive line slides a position when one man is rested, because the lineup builder fills five slots from whoever is available rather than substituting into a specific slot; the naming made that audible rather than causing it, and fixing it changes which lineman blocks which defender and therefore the harness.

Harness and matrix byte-identical, as they were at every step: nothing here touches simulation. Seed replay confirmed unchanged by the reviewer with the naming cycled mid-game. Twenty-five whole games were replayed line by line comparing the stored text against the rebuild: zero differences, and all 58 timeout clauses preserved. Driven in a real browser: the three modes read as intended, A re-reads the last play in each, the substitution list speaks positions, and the quarter is an ordinal again.

## Session 6, August 29, 2026: Milestone 13, the last play and where the ball is

Brian left six new play notes. This milestone is the small one - three voice changes, no simulation math touched, and the harness and matrix verified byte for byte identical before the commit, which was the acceptance test.

S says the last play again. The problem it solves is that C repeats the last report, and the info keys - Z, X, Tab - all legitimately overwrite that buffer, so a coach who checked anything after a play had no way back to the play. S walks the game log back to the most recent action (a snap, kick, or punt) and speaks it with everything that followed - the touchdown and the extra point ride along, because the extra point is a consequence of the score rather than the action a coach is asking about. Everything renders through renderEntry, so the naming and verbosity in force at the moment of asking apply, not the ones in force when the snap ran.

Tab now names the possession outright: "fourth and nineteen. Fairview ball, own eighteen." Brian's own suggested wording, and better than the pronoun form it replaces, because "our own" makes the listener resolve whose ball it is before the yard line means anything; naming the team first leaves nothing to resolve. Own and opponent are relative to the team just named. The every-snap call prompt head keeps its short coach-relative form on purpose - when the game is asking you for a call, you know whose ball it is.

Z remembers across drives, with the wording Brian approved: the per-possession stamps stay (they are what guarantee Z never reports one team's unit as the other's), but a per-team memory now sits behind them, so the first snap of a new drive says "The last time they had the ball, they showed twenty one personnel from the I Formation" instead of pretending the whole night never happened. The memory rides through saves.

## Session 6, Milestone 14: the try

The extra point was the computer's decision even in the mode built to stop the coach for the big ones, which was Brian's third note. The try is now deferred to its own step the same way the kickoff was - tryPAT just marks it pending, stepGame resolves it through a hook with the old deterministic logic as the headless fallback, and the call sites did not change at all. The draws move without reordering, and the harness came out byte for byte identical, which was the acceptance test.

The gating follows the ruling Brian gave before the plan was written: in full control he is asked at every score; on the stop-me setting his coordinator only stops him when a two-point try is genuinely live, which is the same deterministic arithmetic the computer coach and the recommendation both read (fourth quarter, inside ten minutes, down two, down five, down eight, or up one, measured after the six is on the board); fully delegated never asks. Unlike the dead kickoff at zero on the clock, a try at zero is always askable, because the point can decide the game.

Two things rode along. The two-point snap was invisible - the coach heard "two point try fails" with no word of what had been run, because runPlay's two-point path returns before the log push. The try now logs a line of its own, carrying its result so it re-renders under the naming setting, with the down-and-distance head replaced by "Two point try:" since a try is not a first down. And the kicker finally has a name on extra points and field goals - "kicker Ackerman's extra point is good" - which is the first half of the special-teams-name-nobody item.

The subtle piece was overtime. The rotation bookkeeping - who possesses next, whether the game is over - reads the score, and the try now lands its point a step later, so deciding the period off the bare six would end a game the kick was about to tie. The rotation now waits behind an otRotate flag until the try resolves, preserving the old synchronous order exactly, just spread across steps. The auditor then found the hole in that: the controller did not know the waiting step existed, so after every overtime try it asked a phantom question on the dead possession's stale state - "third and goal at their zero" with a full suggestion - and swallowed the answer in the bookkeeping step. One guard in nextPending fixed it, with a regression test that forces overtime and asserts no call prompt ever surfaces while the rotation waits. The auditor's other real finding: S said "Nothing has happened yet" right after the coin toss had been announced, because the toss was not on the action list. It is now. Two pre-existing wrongs were logged rather than fixed, both needing a deliberate engine pass: a false start on a two-point try ends the try instead of replaying it, and try snaps count in the ordinary stats.

Harness and matrix byte for byte identical before and after. Driven in the browser to a real try: "Kick the extra point. ## Session 6, Milestone 15: the defense's fourth down

Brian's first two notes were the same finding from two angles: on the opponent's fourth down his defense had no voice at all. The cause was one branch - nextPending's down-four check sat above the side dispatch and returned auto for any opponent fourth down, so even a go-for-it snap ran without his call, while punt() and fieldGoal() took no defensive input for a call to reach anyway.

The gate restructure reads the opponent's decision, which is honest because fourthDownDecision draws nothing and the unit trotting out is public information - reading it is watching the field, not the call sheet. A go-for-it now falls through to the ordinary defensive flow, which alone fixes "the play just ran". A shown punt unit asks: set up the return (the default, byte for byte the old behaviour), go for the block, or punt safe. A shown field goal unit asks: rush the kick, or field goal safe. Delegation gates like everything else: full control always asks; the stop-me setting interrupts only for a punt in the desperation window, where the block is genuinely the recommended gamble - a field goal's standard call is already the rush, so there is nothing routine to stop for; fully delegated never asks. Kneel-outs stay nobody's question.

The calls have real teeth, authored modest. A committed rush blocks about one punt in twenty-five and one field goal in eighty, measured over three hundred and four hundred forced calls respectively; the price is the return halved and, against a fake, the rush playing as a misaligned defense - which is what an all-out rush is once the ball is not kicked, and reuses the flag resolve.js already models rather than inventing a mechanism. The computer defense makes the same calls from the same public arithmetic, so blocks happen to the coach too. Punts now name the punter and the returner - "punter Ingram punts thirty eight yards, returner Thornton returns seven" - which with the kickers named last milestone closes the special-teams-name-nobody item.

The stale-personnel bug from the session 3 audit rode along, because it belonged in the same seed break: buildSuggestion handed chooseDefense the last formation seen even when, after a turnover, it belonged to the coach's own team. The look is now guarded by whose it was, falling back to the per-team memory the Z key uses. Its effect is confined to controller games - the headless snap path always used the true formation - so it moves no harness number, but it reorders suggestion draws, which is exactly why it waited for a deliberate pass.

The measured cost of the milestone, three hundred even games before and after: 17.7 points a team a game to 17.5, field goals 0.8 to 0.7 (the block existing at all), average margin 13.1 to 12.4, and every header target inside its band. Full numbers in BALANCE_NOTES.md.
